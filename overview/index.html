The Negative-Split Myth
Boston Marathon 2014, every finisher cut in half

31,912 finishers, one piece of advice

803 runners in a field of 31,912 ran the second half faster

Run the second half faster than the first: it is the most repeated pacing advice in marathoning. I took the official timing-mat splits for the whole 2014 Boston Marathon and compared each runner's two halves. 2.5% came home faster than they went out. The median runner's second half was 14% slower than the first.

Start: the question

Good advice, or advice nobody manages to follow?

Hold back early, finish faster, run your best time. Training plans say it and the physiology behind it holds up, yet I had never seen anyone quote how many ordinary runners on an ordinary race day pull it off.

Boston publishes a half-marathon split for every finisher, so the question has a direct answer. Each race splits into the time to the halfway mat and the time from that mat to the line. If the second number is smaller, the runner ran a negative split. The whole analysis is that subtraction, done 31,912 times.

13.1 mi: the answer

Almost nobody speeds up

2.5%

of 31,912 finishers (803 runners) ran the second half faster than the first.

Another 3.9% finished between even and 2% slower, close enough to call flat. The rest of the field, about 94%, gave time back. The whole distribution of slowdowns sits on the slow side of zero, and its peak is at roughly 12 to 15%.

20 mi: who fades

Even pacing goes with being fast

Split the field by finish time and the slowdown grows at every step from front to back: about 4.5% for the 2:00 to 2:30 group, 26.8% for the 5:00 to 6:00 group. The card below gives the median halves for each band.

The direction of cause needs care. Even pacing probably helps, but much of this gap is that fitter runners are further from their limit at halfway. The data shows the association and can't separate the two.

23 mi: a smaller gap

Women paced slightly more evenly

The median slowdown was 13.4% for women and 14.8% for men. Women were a little less likely to run a negative split (2.3% against 2.7%), so the difference lies in how far each group faded. Both differences are small next to the ability effect above.

Limitations: what this doesn't show

One race, one day, one course

This is one edition of one marathon, and Boston's course works against even pacing. It drops for the first few miles and puts the Newton hills between miles 16 and 21, which makes an even split harder to hold. Some of the 14% belongs to the course.

Boston is also a qualifying race, so its field is faster and more experienced than a typical big-city marathon. That should push toward more even pacing, so a general-entry field would probably fade more.

The halfway split is the only checkpoint used. A runner who surged and faded within one half looks the same as one who ran it steadily. Weather on the day is not modeled.

25 mi: what to take from it

Read it backwards

The title invites the reading that negative splitting is a myth and not worth trying. I take the opposite lesson from the data: the runners closest to even pace were the fastest ones, and the field's pacing errors run almost entirely in one direction.

Of 31,912 finishers, almost none started too slowly. Whatever pace feels right at the start line, this field says it is too fast.

Finish: how it was built

Where the numbers come from

Two Python scripts. fetch_data.py downloads the results and analyze.py writes the figures and tables/summary.csv. The raw data is git-ignored and downloaded on demand; the figures and table are committed.

Data
Official 2014 Boston Marathon results, from the public llimllib/bostonmarathon mirror
Sample
31,912 finishers with a valid half-marathon split
Definition
First half is the half-marathon mat time; second half is finish minus half. Ratio is second over first. Negative split means a ratio below 1; even means 1.00 to 1.02
Stack
Python, pandas, NumPy, matplotlib
Reproduce
pip install -r requirements.txt, then python fetch_data.py && python analyze.py
Companion study
Hitting the Wall follows the same field 5 km at a time to find where the fade starts
