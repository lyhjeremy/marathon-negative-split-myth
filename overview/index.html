The Negative-Split Myth
Boston Marathon 2014, every finisher cut in half

31,912 finishers, one piece of advice

803 runners in a field of 31,912 ran the second half faster

Run the second half faster than the first: it is the most repeated pacing advice in marathoning. In the 2014 Boston Marathon, 2.5% of finishers did it. The median runner's second half was 14% slower than the first, and the further back in the field, the bigger the fade.

Start: the question

Good advice, or advice nobody follows?

Training plans say to hold back early and finish faster, and the physiology behind it holds up. I had never seen a count of how many ordinary runners actually manage it.

Boston publishes a half-marathon split for every finisher, so the count is easy to make. Each race splits into the time to the halfway mat and the time from that mat to the line. If the second number is smaller, the runner ran a negative split. The analysis is that subtraction, done 31,912 times.

13.1 mi: the answer

Almost nobody speeds up

The rust bars at the left of the chart are the negative splits: 803 runners. Another 3.9% finished between even and 2% slower. Everyone else, about 94% of the field, gave time back, and the most common fade was 12 to 15%. Pick a finish band to see how the shape moves from the front of the field to the back.

20 mi: who fades

Even pacing goes with being fast

Split the field by finish time and the median slowdown grows at every step, from 4.5% for the 2:00 to 2:30 group to 26.8% for the 5:00 to 6:00 group. Negative splits thin out the same way: 8.2% of the 2:00 to 2:30 group ran one, and 0.5% of the 5:00 to 6:00 group.

The cause runs both ways. Even pacing probably helps, but fitter runners are also further from their limit at halfway. The data shows the association and can't separate the two.

23 mi: a smaller gap

Women paced slightly more evenly

The median slowdown was 13.4% for women and 14.8% for men. Women were a little less likely to run a negative split (2.3% against 2.7%), so the difference is in how far each group faded. Both gaps are small next to the one between the front and the back of the field.

Limitations: what this doesn't show

One race, one day, one course

This is one edition of one marathon, and Boston's course works against even pacing. It drops for the first few miles and puts the Newton hills between miles 16 and 21. Some of the 14% belongs to the course, so a split from a flat race will look better against this field than it would at home.

Boston is also a qualifying race, with a faster and more experienced field than a typical big-city marathon. That should push toward more even pacing, so a general-entry field would probably fade more.

The halfway split is the only checkpoint used. A runner who surged and faded within one half looks the same as one who ran it steadily. Weather on the day is not modeled.

26 mi: what to take from it

Read it backwards

The title invites the reading that negative splitting is a myth and not worth trying. I take the opposite lesson: the runners closest to even pace were the fastest ones, and the field's pacing errors run almost entirely in one direction.

Of 31,912 finishers, almost none started too slowly. Whatever pace feels right at the start line, this field says it is too fast.

Finish: how it was built

Where the numbers come from

fetch_data.py downloads the results and analyze.py writes the figures and tables/summary.csv. export_web.py reads the same data through analyze.load() and writes the band histograms and percentiles this page draws from. The raw data is git-ignored and downloaded on demand.

Data
Official 2014 Boston Marathon results, from the public llimllib/bostonmarathon mirror
Sample
31,912 finishers with a valid half-marathon split
Definition
First half is the half-marathon mat time; second half is finish minus half. Ratio is second over first. Negative split means a ratio below 1; even means 1.00 to 1.02
Stack
Python, pandas, NumPy, matplotlib; the charts here are plain SVG
Reproduce
pip install -r requirements.txt, then python fetch_data.py && python analyze.py && python export_web.py
Companion study
Hitting the Wall follows the same field 5 km at a time to find where the fade starts
